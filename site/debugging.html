Debugging a Lisp machine

A CADR is debugged by another CADR. This page is how MIT did that, and how a board here does it.

The debugger is not a program on a host. It is a second Lisp machine, reaching the first over a cable of twenty-one wires and running bus cycles on it as though it were that machine’s own processor. The program that drives the cable is CC, and it is Lisp software on the debugging machine. Every board here is a debuggee the moment it is powered, which is what a CADR is with nothing set, and one of them can be told to be the debugger instead.

How MIT debugged a CADR

The debugger’s DBGOUT connector goes to the debuggee’s DBGIN connector on its bus interface board. Four wires go one way, sixteen are data lines that either end may drive, and one comes back. The four carry a request, a write line and two address bits, and those two bits are bits 3 and 2 of the debugger’s own Unibus cycle into 0o766100 through 0o766137. So a debugger works the cable by writing four registers of its own.

At the far end a 74S139 turns the two bits into four strobes. One latches sixteen bits of address, one latches three modifier bits, one drives the debuggee’s error status onto the low byte, and one runs a cycle on the debuggee’s Unibus at the address last latched. That last strobe is the whole instrument: everything on that machine’s Unibus is then reachable, main memory included, through its own Unibus map.

How the fabric does it

MIT’s DBGIN logic is in the machine here, and so are the four DBGOUT registers CC writes, so a board can be either end of the cable. With nothing set it is a debuggee, and it answers a debugger exactly as MIT’s board answers one.

There are two ways in and one DBGIN page behind both. The first is a register window on one of the processing system’s general-purpose ports: muir, on the board’s own Arm cores, is a CADR that runs CC, and it puts a whole request into one word and loads the answer back. The second is a ribbon to another board. A board is told which end it is by the console, or by a line in the card’s file of flags.

The cable

A Pmod header has twelve pins: eight signals in two rows of four, a ground in each row and 3.3 V in each row. A cable for this link joins the eight signals and the two grounds and leaves the supply pins open at both ends. The grounds must be joined; the supplies must not, because two boards’ regulators tied together is not something either of them is built for.

A ribbon between two host sockets can also be made the wrong way up, and one was. The header’s two rows are exchanged by it, so each board’s pins 1 to 4 land on the other’s pins 7 to 10. The fabric looks for that rather than trusting the cable: a board told to be the debugger drives nothing at first, listens on both groups of pins, and compensates if it must.

The protocol on the wire

Twenty-one wires do not fit on eight pins, so the levels are sent as frames. Four pins go each way and neither group is ever driven from both ends, which makes the link full duplex with nothing to turn around. One pin of the four is a strobe, and nothing is clocked by it: it is sampled through two flip-flops like any other asynchronous input, and each board keeps a clock of its own.

A frame carries the levels, a two-bit marker reading 01 and one parity bit. The marker tells a frame from a connector with nothing on it, because a payload of all zeros and one of all ones are both legal. The silence after the last beat tells a receiver which beat is beat zero. A frame that fails either moves nothing, and the far end sends the same levels again one frame later.

What crosses is twenty of MIT’s signals one way and nineteen the other. The bus enable and its direction are not among them: they are made on the debugger’s own board and never leave it. The return carries two bits MIT’s cable did not need, saying which bytes of the data lines this end drives, because the status read drives only the low byte and a ribbon has no pull-ups to carry the rest.

What has run on a board

All three pieces have run on silicon. The window has carried CC into a machine that was running Lisp, the ribbon has carried frames between two boards, and CC has crossed the ribbon: the debugger board halting the machine in the other board’s fabric and reading it back, both ways round. What has not crossed it is a write.
